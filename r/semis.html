The Semiconductor Market

AI spending has roughly doubled chip sales in a year. Memory took most of the gain, a handful of suppliers control the bottlenecks, and the market prices the biggest earners as if the cycle will turn.

01

Memory drove the doubling

WSTS put 2025 chip sales at $795.6B and in June forecast $1.51T for 2026, up 90% [1]. After first-half sales of $702B, it raised the 2026 figure to about $1,655B [2]. Most of the increase is memory, forecast to grow about 250% to $804B as AI servers take high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and push up prices for all DRAM [1]. Analog, discretes and sensors grow in the single or low double digits.

02

The supplier layers

Chipmakers bought $135.1B of manufacturing equipment in 2025 [5] and $73.2B of materials, of which $45.8B went into wafer fabs and $27.4B into packaging [6]. SEMI's July forecast has equipment rising 23% to $165.9B in 2026, with wafer fab equipment at $143.9B, test at $15.3B and assembly and packaging at $6.7B [4]. Design software is small by comparison: Cadence, one of the three main EDA vendors, guides to $6.26B to $6.34B of 2026 revenue [27].

03

One foundry makes most of the money

The ten largest contract chipmakers earned a record $53.49B in Q2 2026, and TSMC took 72.5% of it [7]. Samsung (5.9%) and SMIC (5.4%) are a distant second and third. In Q3, TSMC's revenue reached $46.72B, and 79% of wafer revenue came from 7nm and newer processes, including 8% from 2nm in its first full quarter of volume [8][9].

04

Memory: three suppliers and a price spike

HBM is supplied by three firms: SK hynix held 50% of revenue in Q2 2026, Samsung 33% and Micron 18% [10]. Micron's quarter to early September brought in $54.23B, nearly five times the $11.32B of a year earlier, at an 86.8% gross margin [11]. SK hynix posted a 76% operating margin in Q2 [12]. TrendForce still expects conventional DRAM contract prices to rise 10% to 15% in Q4 2026, a slower increase than in Q3 [13].

05

Who buys: data centers first

Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Microsoft now plan roughly $730B of 2026 capital spending between them, and Amazon said higher memory prices were the main reason it raised its plan to about $220B [15][16][17][18]. That money lands at Nvidia, whose data center unit earned $89.0B of its $96.2B quarterly revenue [14]. Gartner estimates AI processors passed $200B of sales in 2025 [3].

TSMC's revenue mix shows the same shift from the factory side. High-performance computing, mostly AI accelerators and server CPUs, rose from 57% of revenue to 68% in a year, while smartphones fell from 30% to 21% [8]. Customers based in North America account for 79% of its revenue [8].

06

Where chips are made

China, Taiwan and Korea bought 79% of all chipmaking equipment in 2025 [5]. China stayed the largest buyer at $49.3B, while Taiwan's spending rose 90% to $31.5B on AI capacity [5]. SIA and BCG estimate that the US share of fab capacity rises from 10% in 2022 to 14% by 2032 as subsidised fabs open, but East Asia still holds most of it [19]. Taiwan was the largest buyer of materials in 2025, at $21.7B [6].

07

Chokepoints

SIA and BCG count more than 50 steps in the chain where one region holds over 65% of the market [19]. The table lists the ones that matter most for leading-edge chips. In each case, losing one supplier or one region would stop production for most of the industry.

ASML management has said its planned 30% capacity increases for 2027 and 2028 will not be enough to meet demand [24]. Because EUV tools set the pace for new leading-edge fabs, this caps how fast foundry capacity can grow regardless of how much the buyers spend.
08

Margins by layer

Profit follows scarcity. Memory makers and Nvidia earn the highest margins right now, TSMC is close behind, and packaging is the lowest-margin layer: ASE's assembly and test unit made a 27.3% gross margin [28]. Analog is steadier: Texas Instruments earned 61% gross and 42% operating margins in Q2 with revenue up 23% [26]. Memory margins swing the most: Micron's gross margin was 38% in its fiscal Q3 2025 [29].

09

Valuation versus growth

The fastest-growing chip companies trade at or below the market multiple. In early October, Micron traded at about 6 times forward earnings and Nvidia at 19.9 times, against 20.3 for the S&P 500 [29]. Investors are pricing peak earnings that they expect to fall when new memory and AI capacity arrives. The broader chip ETF traded at 22.8 times forward earnings in July, slightly above the S&P 500 [30].
