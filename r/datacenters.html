Datacenters and Neoclouds

What an AI datacenter is made of, what a gigawatt costs, where capacity is going, and how the GPU-rental neoclouds pay for it.

Atabak Mehrabani · October 2026 · 9 min read

01

Inside the building

An AI datacenter is five systems stacked together: a power chain from the grid connection down to each rack, cooling that removes almost all of that power as heat, the GPU servers, the network that ties them into one cluster, and the shell around it. What changed with AI is density. One Nvidia GB200 NVL72 rack holds 72 GPUs and draws about 124 kW [3], while older halls were built for 5 to 10 kW per rack [4]. Past roughly 40 kW per rack, air cooling stops being enough and liquid cooling is required [3].

02

What a gigawatt costs

Estimates for 1 GW of AI capacity run from about $35 billion to $60 billion, depending on who counts and which chips are assumed [1][2]. Epoch AI's model of a GB200 campus puts up-front spending at $37.9 billion, of which servers are $21.2 billion [1]. Because servers wear out in about five years and the building lasts fourteen, servers are 60% of the annual cost even though the electricity bill is under $0.6 billion a year [1].

03

Where it is being built

The United States used about 45% of the world's datacenter electricity in 2024, ahead of China at 25% and Europe at 15% [6]. Inside North America the build is concentrated in a few markets. Northern Virginia alone has about 4.5 GW of inventory and 0.2% vacancy, and Atlanta now has the largest construction pipeline at 2.9 GW [8]. Across the main markets, 80% of the space under construction is already leased [8].

04

Power is the binding constraint

US datacenters used 176 TWh in 2023, about 4.4% of national electricity, and the Department of Energy projects 6.7% to 12% by 2028 [7]. The strain shows up first in PJM, the grid operator covering Virginia. Its capacity price went from $28.92 to $333.44 per MW-day in three auctions, and the latest one still fell 6.6 GW short of its reliability target [9][10]. The IEA estimates about 20% of planned datacenter projects could be delayed by grid limits [6].

05

Hyperscaler capex

Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta spent about $413 billion on capex in 2025 and are guiding to roughly $735 to $760 billion for 2026 [11][13]. In the second quarter of 2026 alone the four spent about $172 billion, 77% more than a year earlier [11]. Alphabet raised its range to $195 to $205 billion in July, and Amazon raised to about $220 billion, citing higher memory prices [14][15].

06

The neoclouds

Neoclouds rent GPUs by the hour or, more often now, under multi-year reserved contracts. They sign power and shell leases, buy Nvidia systems with borrowed money, and resell the capacity to AI labs and hyperscalers. CoreWeave had 1.5 GW active and 3.7 GW contracted at the end of June [16]. Crusoe reports 1 GW online and more than 6 GW contracted after raising $3.9 billion at a $30.9 billion valuation [21].

07

Contracts and debt

The business works when a creditworthy customer signs for years of capacity before the GPUs are bought, so lenders can lend against the contract. CoreWeave's OpenAI agreements add up to about $22.4 billion and its Meta agreements to about $35 billion [17][22]. The cost is interest: in Q2 2026 CoreWeave paid $640 million in net interest on $2.58 billion of revenue, which turned a small operating loss into a $626 million net loss [16].

08

The price of a GPU-hour

H100 rental prices fell through 2025 as supply caught up, with marketplace rates near $2 an hour and hyperscaler on-demand rates near $6 [25]. Neocloud rates held around $3.30, and in 2026 they turned up: Silicon Data's neocloud H100 index rose 7% from May to July [26]. Nebius raised its on-demand H100 price from $3.85 to $4.50 an hour on October 1, its second increase this year [27].

GPU rental prices, $ per GPU-hour

Sources: Silicon Data [25] (period medians; June 2025 is a single month); Nebius list prices via Spheron [27]. List prices are on-demand; reserved contracts are usually lower.

A rough check on the economics, using numbers above: one GPU rented at $4.50 for every hour of a year earns about $39,400. Epoch's model puts server cost per GW at $21.2B up front [1], which is why utilization and contract length matter more than list price.
