Buying a Small Business in Canada

Buying a cash-flowing business can beat starting one, but only if you value it on verified numbers and finance it under Canadian rules, which are stricter than the US tools assume.

Atabak Mehrabani · August 2026 · 10 min read

01

Why buy instead of start

When you buy an established business you buy proven demand. Customers already call, payroll is already met and the prices have already been tested. Many small firms in trades, professional services and light manufacturing still run on paper, phone calls and spreadsheets, so there is room to run them better.

Supply is also growing. My notes cite Canadian succession data showing that over 75% of small business owners plan to exit within the decade and fewer than 9% have a formal succession plan. Most of those businesses will never be listed.

The popular version of this thesis is "buy a business and add AI." Be careful with it. AI usually removes a cost rather than creating margin. In the illustration below, automating 30% of admin work adds about $54K to EBITDA. That is useful, but price increases, collections, scheduling and dropping bad customers often do more.

Buy a good business at a fair price first. Treat any AI upside as a bonus, not the reason the deal works. If the deal only works with the AI upside, it is not a deal.
02

What makes a good target

The best candidates have a lot of quoting, scheduling, dispatch and billing work, repeat or contract revenue, and fragmented, non-technical competitors. The owner should be the bottleneck, but not the product. If customers come for the owner personally, the goodwill will not transfer.

Sectors that fit: HVAC, plumbing and electrical, commercial cleaning, landscaping and snow removal, pest control, equipment rental, bookkeeping and payroll firms, insurance brokerages, small logistics and quoting-heavy light manufacturing. I deliberately leave out retail, restaurants and hospitality: thin margins, lease risk and high failure rates.

Customer concentration is the clearest single screen. My scoring uses a graduated scale: under 10% from one customer is clean, 20% to 30% typically costs 0.5x to 1.0x off the multiple, and above 30% most lenders pass.

03

Valuation: a multiple of SDE

At this size, businesses sell on a multiple of SDE: net profit plus the owner's salary, interest, depreciation and genuine one-time or personal expenses. Multiples rise with recurring revenue, a manager in place, a diversified customer base, documented systems and growth. They fall with owner dependence, concentration, cyclicality and old equipment. In an asset deal, inventory and equipment are often priced on top of the goodwill multiple, so confirm what is included early.

The value creation story is simple and slow: buy at about 3x SDE, lift earnings 20% to 30% through operations, and the same multiple applies to a bigger number. A less owner-dependent business may also earn a higher multiple. That takes two to four years, not six months.

04

Financing in Canada

Most deals at this size combine four sources: buyer equity, senior debt, a vendor take-back and sometimes an earnout.

  • CSBFP (Canada Small Business Financing Program): up to $1M term loan plus a $150K line of credit. Within the term loan, at most $500K for equipment and leasehold improvements and at most $150K combined for intangible assets and working capital. Rate capped at prime + 3% (7.45% in August 2026, with prime at 4.45%). Revenue must be $10M or less. It cannot finance a share purchase or bare goodwill with no asset purchase.
  • BDC: lends against goodwill and cash flow, with no share-purchase exclusion. Floating base rate 6.55% (April 2026), typical all-in prime + 1 to prime + 6, down payment guidance 20% to 30%.
  • Vendor take-back (VTB): the seller lends part of the price, commonly 10% to 25% of the price over 3 to 5 years, though some small deals go as high as 50% over up to 7 years. It also keeps the seller invested in a smooth handover.
  • Credit unions: often more flexible than big banks on local operating businesses.
  • Earnout: part of the price depends on results after closing. Useful when buyer and seller disagree on the forecast, if the terms are written carefully.

The goodwill trap. A service business is mostly goodwill. In an asset deal, CSBFP can cover the equipment plus at most $150K of intangibles, so it reaches only a fraction of the price. In my tool's test case, a $700K mostly-goodwill business looks strong on coverage but CSBFP can legally carry only $250K of the $525K of debt structured. 05

The 1.25x DSCR test

The debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) is cash available for debt divided by annual loan payments. Canadian banks want at least 1.25x and prefer 1.35x or more. Credit unions accept 1.20x to 1.25x. The US SBA floor is 1.15x, so a deal a US tool calls financeable can fail in Canada.

The most common error is computing DSCR on SDE. SDE includes the owner's pay, and you still have to pay yourself, or someone, to run the business. The lender's number is Adjusted EBITDA = SDE minus a market-rate salary for the buyer. Using SDE overstates coverage by exactly one salary.

06

Red flags

The headline SDE on a listing is wrong more often than it is right. A dataset of 225+ quality-of-earnings reviews found 57% of deals had SDE overstated by 5% or more, and 19% by more than 25%. Personal vehicles, family on payroll and "one-time" costs that recur every year are where add-backs get inflated. I flag a deal when undocumented add-backs exceed about 25% of SDE.

Bad numbers are not the main cause of failure, though. In interviews with 24 failed acquirers on the Acquiring Minds podcast, about 65% of failures traced to misalignment or dishonesty with the seller.

What goes wrong

Sources: a 225+ engagement quality-of-earnings dataset and Jed Morris's interviews with failed acquirers (Acquiring Minds), as cited in my research.

  • Customers come because of the owner personally
  • Revenue falling more than two years running with no clear reason
  • Litigation, environmental liability or unresolved CRA issues
  • A key employee who could leave with half the customers
  • Dependence on one supplier, licence, or contract up for renewal; a lease that cannot be assigned
  • A seller who will not provide tax returns
  • Listing phrases such as "runs itself", "absentee owner", "turnkey", "must sell", "cash business" or "100% seller financing"; brokers report these are often listed precisely because the claim is false
07

The screening process

A common screening funnel is 100, 50, 10, 1: skim 100 deals, analyze 50, make offers on 10, close 1. With a hit rate near 1%, screening is the bottleneck. I built a private tool, Northscout, to automate the first two stages for Canadian listings.

It collects listings from public Canadian marketplaces, then gives each one two separate scores: a deal score (how good is this if the numbers are true) and a confidence score (how much should you believe the numbers). They are never blended, because a confident-looking estimate should not pass for a fact. Five hard gates cap the deal score at 40: one customer over 50% of revenue, a business under two years old, three straight years of falling revenue, SDE below the minimum ($150K by default) and an excluded category.

One rule is deliberately counterintuitive. Phrases like "no website", "never advertised" or "hasn't raised prices in years" count as upside, but only when cash flow is healthy and stable. On a declining business the same phrases mean neglect and count against it. Deals that survive go to an underwriting workbench that checks the add-backs, the Adjusted EBITDA bridge, the CSBFP caps and the DSCR.

Listings are only part of the picture. Off-market deals are where the edge is: accountants, lawyers, insurance brokers and bankers hear "I'm tired" first. Direct outreach by letter, then phone, then in person, framed around legacy and keeping staff, works better than email.

08

After closing: the first year

Days 1 to 90: change nothing. Talk to every employee and the top 20 customers and learn where the money comes from. Days 90 to 180: fix the boring things, such as prices that have not moved in years, collections and scheduling. Days 180 to 365: document, standardize, digitize, then automate, in that order. The AI uses with the best return are quoting, after-hours call handling, scheduling, invoice processing and follow-ups. Capture baseline metrics before closing, or you will never know what paid off.

This is not passive. Plan for a seller transition of at least 3 months, ideally 6 to 12 with a VTB in place, and retention agreements for key staff signed before closing. Nothing here is legal, tax or financial advice; structure and tax questions need a Canadian accountant and lawyer.
